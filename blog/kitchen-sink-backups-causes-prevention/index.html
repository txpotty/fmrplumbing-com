    Kitchen Sink Backups: Causes, Prevention and Quick Solutions

    By Dana Whitfield · September 9, 2026 · 5 min read

    The kitchen drain takes more abuse than any other line in the house. Food scraps, cooking oil, coffee grounds and dishwasher discharge go down it every day. It gets ignored right up until the basin fills. Kitchen backups are one of the most common calls we run in Nevada, and most of them come back to a short list of causes.

    What Causes Kitchen Sink Backups

    Grease is the main culprit. Bacon fat and pan drippings go down hot and liquid, then set up like candle wax once they hit the cool trap and branch line. Over a few months the fat catches every bit of food that passes and turns into a plug you could cut with a knife. Where the water is hard, calcium scale and soap residue bond with the grease into a crust that hot water alone will not touch.

    Mineral deposits crust over aerators, showerheads and valves, which is why softeners and regular water heater flushing are popular.

    Garbage disposals come next. A disposal grinds food, but it does not make it disappear. Fibrous stuff like celery, corn husks, artichoke leaves and onion skins wraps around the grinding plate and mats up in the drain. Pasta and potato peels swell into a paste. Coffee grounds and eggshells never dissolve and settle in low spots like sand.

    Some causes are harder to spot. Old cast iron and galvanized drain lines rust rough on the inside, and every rough patch is a place for grease to grab. A clogged dishwasher drain hose, or one installed without a high loop, can push water back into the sink. A blocked roof vent slows drainage and makes the sink gurgle. If both sides of a double sink back up together, the clog is past the tee where they join.

    Quick Fixes You Can Try

    Start with the simple stuff before anything stronger. These steps clear the majority of slow or standing sinks:

    • Check the disposal. Kill the power, then use the 1/4 inch hex key in the bottom of the unit to free a jam and push the red reset button on the underside.
    • Plunge with the other side sealed. On a double sink, plug the second drain with a stopper or wet rag so the plunger pressure goes at the clog.
    • Pull the P-trap. Set a bucket underneath, loosen the two slip nuts and clean out the curved trap by hand.
    • Run a hand auger. A small drum auger reaches a few feet into the wall to break up a grease plug.
    • Flush with hot water and dish soap once the clog is open, to push loosened grease further down the line.

    Skip the chemical drain openers. The lye and acid in those bottles generate real heat, enough to soften PVC and damage disposal seals. They also make the drain dangerous for whoever opens it next. If plunging and pulling the trap do not work, the clog is likely in the branch line inside the wall. That takes a longer cable.

    How to Prevent the Next Backup

    Prevention is about what goes down the drain. Pour cooled grease into a can and put it in the trash. A paper towel across a greasy skillet before washing keeps a surprising amount of fat out of the line. Scrape plates into the trash or compost and use the disposal only for small, soft scraps with cold water running. Cold water keeps fat solid so the disposal can chop it and push it along.

    Once a month, fill the sink with hot water and pull the stopper. The full basin draining at once scours the trap. A water softener helps too, because drain walls with less scale give grease less to grab. Houses with repeat backups usually do better on a professional cleaning every year or two, especially when the kitchen line is long or runs into an old cast iron stack.

    When to Call a Professional

    Some signs mean the problem is deeper than the trap. Watch for a sink that clogs again within a few days, water rising in a nearby tub or toilet, or gurgling from the dishwasher. Those point to a branch or main line problem.
