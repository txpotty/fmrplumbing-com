    Hard Water in Nevada: When a Water Softener Pays for Itself

    By Maria Delgado · September 27, 2026 · 6 min read

    Water in much of Nevada is very hard, and scale buildup is one of the most common reasons water heaters and fixtures fail early.

    You know hard water when you see it. Spotted glasses and a crusty faucet base are the usual giveaways, along with a shower door that looks dirty an hour after you scrub it. Many water sources, deep wells and surface reservoirs alike, carry a lot of dissolved calcium and magnesium. Hardness is measured in grains per gallon, and 7 grains or more is considered hard water. What you wipe off the glass is harmless, while the scale you never see is the expensive part.

    What Hard Water Does Inside the Plumbing

    Heat drives minerals out of solution. That is why the water heater takes the worst of it. In a tank heater the scale settles on the bottom and coats the elements, and the burner or elements have to run longer to heat the same 50 gallons. That popping or rumbling sound is water trapped under the sediment layer boiling. On a tankless unit, scale crusts the heat exchanger and can throw error codes within a year or two if nobody descales it.

    Faucet cartridges and shower valves clog up too. As the ports narrow, handles get stiff and the spray pattern turns weak and uneven. Dishwasher and washing machine inlet valves wear the same way, and toilet fill valves and flappers need replacing years ahead of schedule. In older galvanized steel lines, scale and rust together can close off half the inside diameter over a few decades. Water pressure drops accordingly.

    How a Softener Works

    A standard softener works by ion exchange. Water runs through a tank of resin beads that swap sodium or potassium for calcium and magnesium. When the resin is loaded, the control valve runs a regeneration cycle, rinsing the beads with brine from the salt tank and sending the minerals down the drain. A metered valve counts gallons and only regenerates when it needs to, which saves both salt and water compared with a timer.

    Salt-free conditioners are a different animal. They use template assisted crystallization, which converts the hardness into microscopic crystals that pass through without clinging to pipe walls. They need no salt and no drain line, which suits some homeowners. What they do not do is remove the hardness, so you will still see spots on the glasses and the shower door.

    When a Softener Pays for Itself

    A properly sized softener, installed by a plumber with a bypass valve and a drain connection, usually runs somewhere between $1,500 and $3,500. Tank capacity and the quality of the control valve move that number the most. Salt is roughly $5 to $10 a bag. Most households use a bag or two each month. A softener tends to earn its keep when several of these are true:

    • You have replaced a water heater, tank or tankless, earlier than it should have failed.
    • You own a tankless heater and want to protect the heat exchanger.
    • Faucet cartridges, shower valves or fill valves wear out every couple of years.
    • You spend real money on descaler, rinse aid and specialty glass cleaner.
    • You plan to stay in the house five years or longer.
    • Your utility water quality report shows hardness above roughly 10 grains per gallon.

    Run the numbers over the life of the unit. For a lot of Nevada households on hard water, adding several years to a water heater and cutting fixture repairs covers the cost of the softener over its working life. Most tanks and resin beds go 10 to 15 years before they need attention. Worn resin can be replaced without buying a whole new softener.

    Getting the Details Right

    Sizing matters more than brand. Too small a unit regenerates every day or two and goes through salt fast. An oversized one can let the resin sit idle too long between cycles. Before recommending a size, a plumber should test the hardness, check iron levels, count the people in the house and measure peak flow. Most installs are looped so the hose bibs and irrigation stay on hard water, since plants do not care about scale.

    Reverse osmosis at the kitchen sink pairs well with a softener. The RO membrane removes the sodium the softener adds, plus most other dissolved solids, so the water you drink and cook with is cleaner than what comes in. It usually adds a few hours if it goes in the same day as the softener.

    When to Call a Professional

    A rumbling tank or a tankless throwing scale codes. Either of those is a reason to look at softening.
