    Frequently Asked Questions About Basement Sump Pump Installation in Summerlin South

    Replacing a pump in an existing pit usually runs $500 to $1,200 including the pump and check valve. Cutting a new pit through the slab typically costs $1,500 to $3,500, and a battery backup adds roughly $600 to $1,500. The backup is a common add.

    A straight pump replacement generally goes without a permit. New pits, new discharge routes and new circuits may, so we look up the local rules and file for a permit when one is needed.

    We strongly recommend professional installation. The job combines electrical, plumbing and concrete work, and a bad seal on the lid, an undersized pit or a discharge that drains toward the house can leave you worse off than having no pump at all. Errors appear at the worst moment.

    Submersibles sit in the pit, sealed, and run quiet. A pedestal mounts the motor on a column above the water, which makes them cheaper and easier to service but louder, so they work best in an unfinished utility room rather than a finished family room. Both move water fine.

    Every two to three months, and before and after the wet season. Pour a five gallon bucket of water into the pit, then watch the float lift, the pump start, the water drop, and the pump shut off cleanly without humming or rattling. It takes a minute.

    Once a year is enough for most pumps, ideally before the wet season. A tune-up covers the float, the pit, the check valve and the discharge, and we confirm the discharge outlet outside is open, since a clogged outlet makes the pump run hard against a blocked line. It takes under an hour.

    Watch for constant running, rapid cycling or grinding noises. Rust stains and a float that sticks are warning signs too, as is water sitting above the switch level without the pump starting. Do not wait for the storm.
