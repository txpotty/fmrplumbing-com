    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in Summerlin South, NV

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. A jetter takes it off the pipe wall.     Grease Line Jetting in Summerlin South

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Summerlin South

    Most visits in Summerlin South run somewhere between $200 and $800, set mostly by trap size and how long it has gone since the last pump-out. Hard access adds time. A trap behind a walk-in that needs 80 feet of hose costs more than one sitting next to the curb. We quote before starting.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    Zurn, Schier, Watts and Big Dipper units turn up across Nevada every week, along with Rockford traps and older steel boxes from makers that are long gone. Parts are usually available. For discontinued units we can often fit a universal baffle or flow control, or quote a replacement if the body has rusted through.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. The disposal is the part that gets people fined. Many programs want a licensed hauler's record for every cleaning, and a kitchen doing its own has nothing to show the inspector.

    Short term, slow drains and smell. Longer term, a hardened grease cap that can take hours to break up. Hydrogen sulfide from the rotting grease also attacks concrete, so interceptors that go unpumped for years often need new baffles and risers well before their time.

    Two things, a full pump-out and a trap that drains properly when we leave. We do not skim and call it done. Should a problem tied to our work show up later, the crew returns to fix it, and the depth readings on the sheet show how we left the trap.

    Slow sinks come first. Gurgling floor drains and a sour smell by the dish station usually follow. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.

    Under-sink traps take 30 to 60 minutes. Plan one to three hours for an outdoor interceptor. Big tanks with a hardened cap take the longest, since the cap has to be broken up with a pressure wand before the hose will pull it.
